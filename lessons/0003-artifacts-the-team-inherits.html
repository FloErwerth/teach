Lesson 03 · Human-in-the-loop AI coding

Leave artifacts the team inherits

A grill that ends in your head ends with you. The two things that survive it — a tightened CONTEXT.md and the occasional ADR — are what carry your hard-won understanding to the colleague reviewing your PR.

Mission: the rationale arrives before the reviewer does

Last lesson, in one breath

Before clicking anything: in Lesson 2 you sorted every question into three buckets. Which bucket holds the questions only you can answer — the ones your whole session exists for? Answer from memory, then check the quiz.

Where the understanding leaks out

You run a sharp grill. The fuzzy terms get decided, the edge cases get pinned, the trade-off gets made. Then you implement, open the PR — and all of that lives nowhere but in a scrolled-past chat transcript. Your reviewer re-derives it from scratch, in comments. The session saved you three rounds and cost them three anyway.

The fix is the "with docs" half of the skill, and it is not bookkeeping. The artifacts are how the context you front-loaded reaches the next human — so the rationale lands on their desk before they start typing questions.

You don't write these — the agent does

This is the part people miss: you never sit down and author CONTEXT.md or an ADR yourself. The agent writes them for you, inline, during the grilling session — the moment a term gets pinned or a trade-off gets made, the skill drafts the entry. Your job as the prompter is not to type the doc; it's to steer and approve what the agent proposes — confirm the wording, correct a definition that drifted, or decline an ADR that doesn't earn its place. The documents are a by-product of the conversation, not homework you do afterward.

Two artifacts, two different jobs — never blur them

CONTEXT.md captures language. It is a glossary and nothing else: the canonical term for each concept, what it is, and the words to avoid. An ADR captures a decision. It records that a hard-to-reverse trade-off was made, and why. A term is not a decision; a decision is not a term. Routing each resolution to the right artifact is the whole skill of "with docs."

CONTEXT.md: a glossary, ruthlessly

The format (CONTEXT-FORMAT.md, your primary source) is tighter than it looks, and the constraints are the point:

  1. Define what it IS, not what it does. One or two sentences. The moment a definition starts describing behaviour or flow, you've drifted into spec — pull back.
  2. Be opinionated. When several words mean the same thing, pick the best one and bury the rest under _Avoid_. A glossary that lists synonyms neutrally has decided nothing.
  3. Only project-specific terms. If it's a general programming concept (timeout, retry, cache), it doesn't belong even if you use it constantly. Ask: is this unique to this domain? Only then does it earn a line.
**Trip**:
A planned multi-stop journey a user saves and syncs across devices.
_Avoid_: Route, itinerary, plan
No implementation details. Not a spec, not a scratch pad. The discipline of keeping CONTEXT.md empty of "how" is exactly what keeps the "what" readable by everyone.

ADRs: sparingly, and only when all three hold

The skill offers an ADR only when all three of these are true (ADR-FORMAT.md):

  1. Hard to reverse — changing your mind later carries real cost.
  2. Surprising without context — a future reader will look at the code and ask "why on earth did they do it this way?"
  3. The result of a real trade-off — there were genuine alternatives and you chose one for specific reasons.

Miss any one and you skip it. Easy to reverse? You'll just reverse it. Not surprising? Nobody wonders why. No real alternative? There's nothing to record beyond "we did the obvious thing." And when one does qualify, keep it tiny — one to three sentences. The value is recording that a choice was made and why, not filling out sections.

The test in one example

"We picked Trip over Route" → glossary, not an ADR. It's a term. "We store trips in SQLite, not the existing Realm store, because they must survive a cold launch and Realm's migration cost was too high" → ADR. Hard to reverse, genuinely surprising to the next engineer, and a real trade-off. Same session, two artifacts.

The artifact you'll be tempted to throw away

The glossary and the ADR are the artifacts that leave the session. But the grilling context window is itself a dense, valuable artifact — a buffer packed with decisions you paid real tokens to reach. The classic waste is to finish a sharp grill, /clear, and start implementation on a blank slate. You've just binned the work.

Every decision becomes code, a glossary line, or an ADR — before you clear

You have two honest exits from a grill. Implement in the same session, carrying the decisions live into the code (that's Lesson 4). Or, if you must hand off, first make sure every decision has landed somewhere durable — a CONTEXT.md term, an ADR, or the written plan — and only then clear. Nothing of value should survive only in a transcript you're about to scroll past.

Your win for today

Make one artifact survive your next grill

  1. Run a real grill (Lessons 1–2). When a term gets decided, watch the skill write it into CONTEXT.md — then check it against the three rules: is it tight, opinionated, and free of "how"? Fix it on the spot if not.
  2. Catch the one decision in the session that passes all three ADR tests. If the skill doesn't offer an ADR for it, ask it to. If it offers one for something that fails the test, decline and say which test it misses.
  3. Before opening your PR, link the ADR (or the changed glossary lines) in the description. Notice whether a comment you'd normally expect simply… doesn't arrive.
