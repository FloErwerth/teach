Local review — read the diff like a senior

Green CI and a feature that works is not a reviewable PR. Before any colleague sees it, run the diff through Claude Code's /code-review — and adjudicate what it finds — as if it were a junior's PR you'd been handed cold. The tool does the line-by-line sweep; you make the calls it can't.

The trap: "CI is green, ship it"

The pipeline passes. The feature works on your simulator. So you open the PR and tag a reviewer. But CI only checks the machine-checkable — types compile, tests pass, lint is clean. It says nothing about whether the diff matches the ticket, whether it uses your decided language, whether the edges you fought to specify actually got coded, or whether the tests test anything at all.

Skip local review and your colleague becomes your first reviewer — the most expensive one on the chain. Every issue they catch is a comment, a round-trip, a context-reload on both desks. That is precisely the downstream cost you ran the grill to move upstream. Hand the diff over unread and you quietly undo it.

The shift: drive the review, don't do the scan by hand

The trap is author's bias — "I described it, so I trust it." But you wrote the intent; the model wrote the syntax. Those are different authors, so the syntax needs an adversarial audit — and reading every line yourself is the slow, lossy way to do it. Hand the mechanical sweep to /code-review, run hard, and spend your attention where the tool is blind: does the diff match the ticket, your decided language, the edges you grilled? The model optimises for plausible; your job is to judge where plausible isn't correct — not to be the scanner.

Run /code-review first — at high effort or more

Before you read a line yourself, put the whole diff through /code-review (Claude Code's built-in review skill) at at least high effort — a low-effort pass on a handoff diff is barely a pass. Read its findings as an adversary and decide each one — it flags, you adjudicate; never auto-accept.

Three checks the tool can't make for you

/code-review doesn't know your ticket, your CONTEXT.md, or what you pinned in the grill. These stay yours — and they're where your attention goes once the mechanical sweep is done. None of them are things a pipeline, or the tool, will catch.

Scope creep — did it touch more than the ticket?

Drive-by refactors, reformatted files you never asked it to format, an unrelated edit it "noticed while it was in there." Each one buries the real change and forces your reviewer to separate signal from noise. If a hunk isn't the ticket, revert it or split it out.

Decided language and decisions honored

Does the diff use the canonical CONTEXT.md terms, or did it slip back to a synonym you already rejected? Does it respect the ADRs you recorded? A grilled decision that the implementation quietly contradicts is worse than no decision — it reads as agreed when it isn't. (Callback to Lessons 3–4.)

Edge cases and error paths

Find the edges you pinned in the grill and check each one is in the diff. The model reliably nails the happy path and silently drops the error path, the empty state, the offline case — the exact edges you spent the session specifying. They don't show up as failures; they show up as nothing.

The grill front-loaded the thinking; controlled implementation front-loaded the catching during; local review is the last catch before handoff. Every issue you find here is a review comment your colleague never has to write.

Your win for today

Locally review your Lesson 4 diff before the PR exists

Take the change you implemented under your own hand in Lesson 4 and run it through the checklist — before you open the PR:

  1. Run /code-review at at least high effort over the diff first — that's your primary sweep, run before a colleague or CodeRabbit ever sees it. Read its findings as an adversary and decide each one; don't auto-accept.
  2. Then run the three checks the tool can't make. Catch one bit of scope creep — a hunk that isn't the ticket — and split it out. Find one decided CONTEXT.md term it dropped, or one grilled edge missing from the diff. Pick one test and confirm it would actually fail if the logic broke.
  3. Fix what surfaced — and you decide how. A finding that reopens a real decision goes back through a grill (Lessons 1–2); a finding small enough to fix directly you just fix. That call is yours as the reviewee, not the tool's.
  4. Then run /code-review again on the corrected diff — a fix can introduce its own issue, so the loop closes only when a clean pass comes back. Re-review, fix, re-review until it does; then you're ready to hand off.

Bring me what you caught. Once the diff is reviewed and those adjustments are in, the ticket is ready for the next step in your Scrum flow — open the PR, move the card to review, hand it to your colleague. Local review is the gate it passes through to get there; clear it, and the work moves on.

/code-review does the heavy scanning so you don't read every line by hand — but it can't judge your diff against your ticket, your decided language, or the edges you grilled. That judgment, and the call on every finding it raises, stays the human's: it's the practice this lesson teaches, the same way controlled implementation was in Lesson 4. Anchor it in the mission's constraint — MISSION.md, the line that says review AI-generated diffs against a personal checklist and catch issues before a colleague ever sees them.
